I competed in this contest during the October holiday. The first five problems felt easy, but I could not solve the sixth and only earned a few points with brute force.

After reading the editorial, I saw the sixth problem’s idea was something I had not considered. I learned from it, though it was not very difficult.

The seventh problem seemed to require a link-cut tree. I have not learned that yet, so I will return to it later.

Problem link

The first three problems were too straightforward to write about.

4.Bob’s Game

Given an $n * n$ grid with some unreachable cells and several kings, each king may move up, left, or up-left. It stops at the top-left corner. Determine the winner and the number of winning first moves.

Each king is independent, so compute every king’s Sprague–Grundy value and XOR them. Then check moves in $O(3n)$.

5.Black White Tree

Given a black-and-white tree, cut a subtree so that the absolute difference between black and white vertices is maximised. Return that maximum and the possible root choices.

Assign black vertices 1 and white vertices -1; a subtree’s sum is the difference.

dp[x][1] is the largest difference when selecting this vertex; dp[x][0] is the largest difference when not selecting it.

After finding the branch with the largest difference, reroot there, search again, and select greedily.

6.March of the King

On an $8 * 8$ chessboard, find a string of length 11 without revisiting any cell. Count the solutions.

$7^{11}$ is too large.

I had discussed this with others before: use meet-in-the-middle, hash the recorded paths, then apply inclusion–exclusion when counting.
